Tamas Varga In most game-theoretic models, the payoff function is fixed. However, in biological evolution, not only the individuals' behavioral strategies may change, but also their ability to implement a given strategy effectively (for example, in the case of a physically stronger individual, an aggressive strategy may become more effective). This may lead to situations in which, even under identical behavior, payoffs are not fixed; that is, an additional strategic component emerges that influences the payoff function.We refer to this component as a trait, which may represent, for instance, an individual's physical strength, size, speed, or hormone levels. The trait, together with the behavioral strategy, constitutes the individual's overall strategy (phenotype).We present a game-theoretic model in which the elements of the payoff matrix depend on individuals' traits, and we illustrate with an example how this framework allows not only the behavioral strategy but also the payoff matrix itself to evolve. As a consequence, the nature of the game may change (for example, transforming from a prisoner's Dilemma into a harmony game).
Doug Yu It is an old truism in management that "You only get what you measure". In conservation, how do we get businesses and governments to be nature-friendly? We have to measure their impacts, both positive and negative, and apply incentives so that we "shift global financial flows away from nature-negative outcomes and toward nature-positive outcomes" (tnfd.global). To understand what to measure, we need a principal-agent model, and I introduce one that lays out four criteria needed for an effective biodiversity metric. If time permits, I will briefly summarise some old work on principal-agent models for mutualism.

Thomas Schmickl, Martin Stefanec Over the past two decades, the Artificial Life Lab at the University of Graz has been connecting biology and mechatronic (robotic) technology. The Western honeybees have always been at the center of our biological research in Graz since almost a century. A honeybee colony is more than just a collection of individuals: it is a cohesive social system with complex, collective behaviors that self-regulate the colony. Our research journey began with simple questions: How do bees react to a set of stimuli locally emitted in the hive? What if the emitter was a “robot”, under what conditions did they accept such systems as part of their environment? This informed the development of robots and sensor systems, that tap into a colony’s communication, can influence (modulate) collective decision-making, monitor and analyze colony dynamics and ultimately can be seamlessly integrated into the animals' surroundings. Each technical step raised new biological questions. Biological research drove technology, and technology innovation in turn drove biological research forward, creating a continuous feedback loop between living systems and engineered machines.Our scientific endeavor also represents a broader transition in research: The machines were initially tools for specific experiments. Now, however, the biohybrid technology has become the foundation of practical applications: Future systems may change honeybee colony monitoring, protection, and management, contributing to more sustainable pollination and ecosystem stewardship.Such a development is not limited to honeybees. Technologies that sense, interpret, and interact with a collective biological system could provide new approaches for studying and supporting other social insects, animal groups, and potentially ecological communities. Biohybrid research may thus evolve from engineering machines that study life toward technologies that participate, carefully and adaptively, in living systems.
